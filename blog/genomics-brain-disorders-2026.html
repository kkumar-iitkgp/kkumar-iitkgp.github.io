Genetics is only the starting point

October 31, 2026

What stayed with me from Genomics of Brain Disorders 2026, Wellcome Genome Campus, 16–18 September 2026.

I attended Genomics of Brain Disorders 2026 with the support of a bursary from Wellcome Connecting Science, and also had the opportunity to present my work on how gene deletions and duplications can affect brain and behaviour through different biological functions. My talk was part of the opening session, Genes in Context: When and Where Genetic Risk Acts, which was closely aligned with the question behind my own work. That made me especially excited for the meeting: I wanted to see how researchers working at very different levels, from human genetics to cells, brain imaging and clinical studies, were approaching the same broader problem.

The meeting brought together human genetics, psychiatry, single-cell biology, cellular models, neuroimaging and new computational approaches. For me, its strength was not one field or one result, but seeing these different ways of studying brain disorders in the same room. Five moments stayed with me most.

Genetics gives us a strong starting point. The harder question is how to connect a genetic finding to biology, development, measurable outcomes and, eventually, the person carrying the variant.

A conversation that changed how I frame the question

One of my most useful conversations began with my own CNV talk and then moved far beyond it. I spoke with Eli Stahl from the Regeneron Genetics Center about how research questions are taken forward across different stages, and how teams with different expertise take the lead as a project develops. We discussed the importance of defining a reliable endpoint or biomarker early, his interest in EEG, and how new computational and AI approaches may fit into this process.

What I took from this was very practical. I often begin from the genetics and then ask what biology it points to. The conversation made me think more about the other direction too: what would be a meaningful outcome at the end, and can that shape the research question from the beginning? In my own work, this means involving people who work directly with CNV carriers earlier, including researchers who know the clinical assessments, developmental measures and the questions families actually ask. Eli has worked across academic genetics and industry, and that combination made his perspective particularly useful to me.

Two keynotes that framed the meeting

The two keynote talks were the scientific highlights for me, and they also made a fitting start and end to the meeting. Matthew Hurles opened with the history and continuing work of the Deciphering Developmental Disorders programme and related studies. The scale of the effort was impressive, but what I found most useful was seeing how the questions have changed over time: from finding disease-associated genes, to understanding variants that remain difficult to interpret, developmental trajectories, cellular effects and the large variability between people.

Manolis Kellis closed the meeting from a very different angle. He moved from single-cell atlases and disease-associated cellular states to data-driven models, patient heterogeneity and the possible role of foundation models and AI. One example stayed with me. He used the change from Newton's description of gravity to Einstein's description of space-time to make a point about representation: sometimes progress comes from changing how a problem is represented, not only from adding more data to the same description.

It made me think about my own research. A genetic variant or a list of affected genes may not always be the most useful representation; the biological systems through which those genes act may sometimes be more informative.

Where the meeting felt most alive

The event felt most alive in the spaces between the formal talks. The programme moved quickly from genetics to cellular models, spatial biology, imaging, patient stratification and computation, and the poster sessions made it easy to continue those discussions. The Wellcome Genome Campus also changed the feel of the meeting. A short morning or evening walk through the quiet campus was genuinely refreshing and made it easier to return to the science with a clear head.

A much smaller moment also captured the care behind the meeting. At the end of the conference, I saw members of the organising team running after an airport coach so that a participant who had been left behind could get on. It was a simple thing, but it summed up the effort that had gone into the event and the way participants were looked after throughout the three days.

The takeaway I am bringing back

The main thing I will bring back is to think about the full path from a genetic finding to something that can eventually be measured and understood in people. Genetics is a strong starting point, but it is only the start. The talks showed many routes from genetics towards mechanism: cellular models, transcriptomics, spatial biology, brain imaging and longitudinal phenotyping. My conversation with Eli added another question: what is the endpoint, and are we choosing it early enough?

For my own work on CNVs, I want to keep the population-scale genetics that gives statistical power, but connect it more deliberately to researchers and cohorts that work closely with carriers. This should help decide which outcomes are worth predicting, which intermediate measures are realistic, and which findings would actually justify further mechanistic or clinical study.

Leaving with a different sense of scale

I left the meeting thinking differently about scale. Large genetic studies have made it possible to find many variants associated with brain disorders. Single-cell and other molecular approaches now let us look much more closely at what those variants may do. At the same time, large population cohorts allow us to look for general patterns across variants and phenotypes. The challenge is to connect these levels without losing sight of the person who carries the variant.

That is probably why the meeting felt so energising to me. Matthew Hurles showed what years of careful human genetics can build. Manolis Kellis pointed towards new ways of organising and learning from increasingly complex data. The many talks and conversations between them showed the work needed to connect those two ends. I came away with new ideas for my own programme, but also with a clearer sense that the best questions will need genetics, biology, clinical knowledge and computation to speak to each other from the start.

I am very grateful to Wellcome Connecting Science for the bursary and for the opportunity to take part in the meeting, present my work, and have these conversations.

These are personal reflections from the meeting rather than a comprehensive summary of the programme.
